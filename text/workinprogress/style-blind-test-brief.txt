TASK: Rewrite three passages of a text-adventure game in prose form.

Work only from this brief. Do not read any other files, repos, or knowledge.
This is a style test.

Context (all you need to know): A fantasy game, Dark Age setting,
Norse-flavored refugees fleeing an empire by boat. Second-person, present
tense. <A>, <B>, <C>, <D> are placeholders for the names of the player's
four friends - use them as they are; never use gendered pronouns for these
four. The player's character is a silent protagonist, present but rarely
described. The player's own speech may be described briefly but not quoted
at length.

Style rules:
1. Elevated, dignified register - but no archaic words, no modern slang, no
   "thee/save/'tis"-style costume.
2. Everything in present tense, always.
3. Emotions are never named directly. They are shown through image, action,
   or silence. (Not: "you feel dread." Instead: show what the dread does.)
4. Nature has agency: the sea, the storm, the sun, the birds act and take
   and give. The characters' own saying is "The sea gives, and the sea
   takes."
5. Wonder comes from precise observation presented as remarkable - a
   noticing eye - not from ornament or exclamations.
6. Similes are rationed: at most one per passage, and it must carry weight.
   Every metaphor and simile must be one the characters themselves could
   have imagined - drawn from their world: sea, weather, fire, tools,
   animals, gods, seafaring. Nothing from outside their world, nothing cute
   or sentimental.
7. Sentence music: flowing periods; parallel structure ("and X, and Y, and
   Z") reserved for the gravest beats.
8. Focus on behavior, speech, and mood. Minimal physical description of
   people.
9. 100-250 words per passage.

Passage 1 (opening scene of the game): The sun is setting on another day at
sea. This is the last boat: old, damaged, crowded with refugees and
everything they own. The sail was destroyed days ago; everyone rows in
turns. Everyone is tired. Little fresh water except rain. No land in sight,
no way back. By the player's bench rest three friends, A, B, and C. A fourth
friend, D, sleeps under a tarp; the player decides not to wake D. The
player's own turn at the oars is over; behind them the next rowers settle in.

Passage 2 (dialog scene): C's turn at the oars ends. C sits down with the
player under a shared blanket and watches the first stars come out between
the clouds. C says: "I have been counting the days. And the ships. Three
left home together. The storm took the Wave Dancer. The Emperor's warship
took the Dawn Chaser - but her people were pulled onto this boat, so the
sea did not get them all. And I have been watching the stars. If the maps I
remember are right, there is a coast south of us. If they are wrong, there
is nothing. But the birds went south yesterday, all of them, and the water
is greener now. Birds do not fly over nothing. Green water is shallow
water. I think we are close. I cannot promise it."

Passage 3 (action scene): A shipwreck at night in a storm. The player reaches
for B, who is physically exhausted from days of rowing and has nothing
left. B gasps "Go." The player refuses to leave, locks an arm around B, and
kicks for the shore through the waves, hauling B's dead weight. It is slow
and heavy; the player swallows seawater twice. B holds on to the player;
the player holds on to B; the shore comes anyway.

Write the three rewritten passages. Nothing else.



---

The sun goes down as it has gone down every day since the sea took the sail: unhurried, keeping a time that is not yours. This is the last boat. She is old, and the water has been working at her seams, and she carries all that remains — people, tools, rain-butts, sleeping children, everything the Empire has not taken and the sea has not claimed. Fresh water is what the rain gives, and the rain gives grudgingly. There is no land in sight. The way back is also water.

Your turn at the oars ends. Behind you the next rowers settle onto the benches and take up the stroke, and the boat carries it on. By your bench A, B and C rest in the last of the light, speaking little, saving breath as they save the water. Under the tarp D sleeps on. You look at the tarp, and let morning be soon enough. The sea gives, and the sea takes. This evening it takes only the light.

---

C's turn at the oars ends. C comes and sits beside you, and you spread the blanket over you both, and for a while neither of you says anything. Between the ragged clouds the first stars come out, one, then another, each one placed rather than found.

C speaks without turning from the water. "I have been counting the days. And the ships. Three left home together. The storm took the Wave Dancer. The Emperor's warship took the Dawn Chaser — but her people were pulled onto this boat, so the sea did not get them all." The oars behind you keep their slow count; the water slides past, dark and patient. "And I have been watching the stars. If the maps I remember are right, there is a coast south of us. If they are wrong, there is nothing." C's voice stays level, a line paid out carefully. "But the birds went south yesterday, all of them, and the water is greener now. Birds do not fly over nothing. Green water is shallow water. I think we are close. I cannot promise it."

You answer with what you have, which is little: that you believe the birds, and that when the oars come round to you, you will row. C takes it. Under the blanket the counting goes on, and the stars keep coming.

---

Night, and the storm has the boat in its hands. It takes her apart plank by plank, without pause. You are in the water before you understand that the water has you. You reach for B and find a sleeve, then an arm, then all of B. There is nothing left in B — the days at the oars took it, and the sea has had the rest. B gasps once: "Go." One word, given up like a last coin.

You do not go. You lock an arm around B and turn toward where the shore should be, though the dark offers nothing, and you kick, and pull, and kick. The waves lift you and lie down on you. Twice the sea gets into your mouth, salt and burning; twice you give it back and keep kicking. B holds on to you, and you hold on to B, and the shore comes anyway.
