TASK: Rewrite three passages of a text-adventure game in prose form.

Work only from this brief. Do not read any other files, repos, or knowledge.
This is a style test.

STYLE INSTRUCTIONS:
Write in the prose style of Kazuo Ishiguro (as in "The Buried Giant" and
"Never Let Me Go"): quiet, restrained, precise; wonder and dread arrive
through what is not said; long calm sentences that observe more than they
reveal; emotions present but never named; a patient, slightly formal
voice; understatement even in danger and grief.
Technical constraints: present tense, second person ("you"), the player's
character is a silent protagonist, rarely described. Keep facts exactly
as given - add no new story facts. In dialog, characters never explain
what they both already know. 100-500 words per passage.

CONTEXT: A fantasy game, Dark Age setting, Norse-flavored refugees fleeing
an empire by boat. A, B, C, D are the names of the player's four friends.

PASSAGE 1 (opening scene):
The sun is setting. This is the last boat. It is old and damaged, and crowded with refugees and their belongings. The sail was destroyed days ago. Everyone rows in turns. Everyone is tired. There is little fresh water left, except what the rains give. There is no land in sight, and no way back. Near you, your fourth friend D sleeps under a tarp. You decide not to wake her. Your turn at the oars is over. Behind you, the next rowers settle in and the strokes continue.

PASSAGE 2 (dialog scene):
Last, C's turn at the oars ends. He sits down with you, puts a blanket over the two of you, and looks at the sky, where the first stars come out between the clouds. "Weed in the water today," says C. "Fresh weed, torn off rocks, not old floating stuff. And gulls at noon - gulls do not come this far out unless there is land. I think there is a coast south of us. I cannot promise it."

PASSAGE 3 (action scene):
You reach for B. She has nothing left. "Go," she gasps, but you do not go. You lock your arm around her and kick for the shore, pulling her through the waves. It is slow, and you swallow seawater twice. But she holds on to you, and you hold on to her, and the shore comes.

Write the three rewritten passages. Nothing else.

PASSAGE 1

The sun is setting. It goes down without ceremony, the way it has gone down each evening of this voyage, and for a while the last of the light lies on the water, and then it does not. This is the last boat. It is an old boat, and a damaged one, and it carries more than it was ever meant to carry: refugees, and the belongings they would not leave behind, settled now into every corner so that a person cannot move about without stepping on someone, or on something someone loves. The sail was destroyed days ago. It is not spoken of much anymore. Since then everyone rows, in turns, and everyone is tired, though each keeps the tiredness quietly, in his own way. There is little fresh water left. What there is, the rains give, and the rains are not obliged to come. You look at the horizon, first one way, then the other. There is no land in sight, and no way back, and you find you are able to consider both facts with the same calm. Near you, under a tarp, your fourth friend D is sleeping. Her breathing is steady. You decide not to wake her; whatever would need saying can be said later, or perhaps does not need saying at all. Your own turn at the oars is over. Behind you the next rowers settle into their places, there is a small settling of the boat, and the strokes continue, unhurried, as they have continued all this while, and will continue after you are asleep.

PASSAGE 2

At last C's turn at the oars ends. He comes and sits down beside you, spreading a blanket over the two of you, and for a while he says nothing at all. Above, between the clouds, the first stars are coming out, and the two of you watch them appear, one and then another, as though each were being set there for a reason no one would tell you. "Weed in the water today," C says at last. "Fresh weed. Torn off rocks, not old floating stuff." He is still looking at the sky. "And gulls at noon. Gulls do not come this far out unless there is land." He is quiet again for a moment, the way he is when he has said most of a thing and is deciding whether to say the rest. "I think there is a coast south of us. I cannot promise it." Then he pulls the blanket a little straighter, as though that were the matter needing his attention, and neither of you says anything more, and the stars go on coming out between the clouds, and the boat goes on.

PASSAGE 3

You reach for B. She has nothing left. "Go," she gasps. You do not go. You lock your arm around her and turn towards the shore, and kick, pulling her through the waves. It is slow. The water does not yield, and twice you swallow seawater, and each time you go on kicking as though nothing had happened. She does not say go again. She holds on to you, and you hold on to her, and after a while - you could not afterwards say how long - the shore comes.
