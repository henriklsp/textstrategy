TASK: Rewrite three passages of a text-adventure game in prose form.

Work only from this brief. Do not read any other files, repos, or knowledge.
This is a style test.

STYLE INSTRUCTIONS:
Rewrite all basic text in prose form.
Aim for a mythical style inspired by fantasy authors, such as J.R.R.
Tolkien, U.K. Le Guin, N.K. Jemisin. Include sensory detail and vivid
impressions of scenery, atmosphere, and nature, and objects. Use scenic
descriptions inspired by David Attenborough, especially for passages
setting the scene or depictions of nature, invoking a sense of wonder.
The goal is to make the reader feel that they are experiencing
extraordinary places and events first hand, and that their deeds and
adventures will become legendary.
Ethical and moral themes revolve around human emotions, quirks and vices
such as temptation, pride, courage, folly and hope and the dream of
freedom.
The story is always told from the player's perspective. The player as
main character is referred to as 'you'. The reader only knows what the
main character can see, hear and feel and remember. The player's
character is mostly a silent protagonist, rarely described, but always
present.
Be careful not to describe facts which may contradict the actual game
state. Keep descriptions of person physical traits to a minimum, but
focus on their behaviour, actions, speech, and mood. Use role traits as
guidelines for personalities.
In dialog, be sure to check what each character knows. When giving
information to the player that the players character ('you') already
knows they will talk accordingly.
Always write in the present tense, except in dialog when characters may
speak in past, present or future tense.
Aim for 100 to 500 words per section.

CONTEXT: A fantasy game, Dark Age setting, Norse-flavored refugees
fleeing an empire by boat. A, B, C, D are the names of the player's four
friends.

PASSAGE 1 (opening scene):
The sun is setting. This is the last boat. It is old and damaged, and crowded with refugees and their belongings. The sail was destroyed days ago. Everyone rows in turns. Everyone is tired. There is little fresh water left, except what the rains give. There is no land in sight, and no way back. Near you, your fourth friend D sleeps under a tarp. You decide not to wake her. Your turn at the oars is over. Behind you, the next rowers settle in and the strokes continue.

PASSAGE 2 (dialog scene):
Last, C's turn at the oars ends. He sits down with you, puts a blanket over the two of you, and looks at the sky, where the first stars come out between the clouds. "Weed in the water today," says C. "Fresh weed, torn off rocks, not old floating stuff. And gulls at noon - gulls do not come this far out unless there is land. I think there is a coast south of us. I cannot promise it."

PASSAGE 3 (action scene):
You reach for B. She has nothing left. "Go," she gasps, but you do not go. You lock your arm around her and kick for the shore, pulling her through the waves. It is slow, and you swallow seawater twice. But she holds on to you, and you hold on to her, and the shore comes.

Write the three rewritten passages. Nothing else.

REWRITTEN PASSAGE 1 (opening scene):

The sun goes down in fire and rust, and the sea takes its colours as a dying man takes his last breath - slowly, and without mercy. This is the last boat. She is an old vessel, this one, patched and repatched, her timbers grey and salt-bitten as whalebone, and she groans at every stroke like a grandmother made to carry a giant's load. Of her sail nothing remains but memory and a few charred lashings; days ago the wind tore it, or the empire's fire did, and since then the boat goes where arms take her. And the arms are many, and the arms are weary. Refugees sit shoulder to shoulder along her length, among bundles and chests and cooking-pots and children, all that could be snatched from a burning shore and carried into exile.

You let the oar slide from your blistered hands. Your turn is done. The sea stretches away on every side, heaving slow and vast under the burning sky, and there is no land in it - no smudge of smoke, no bird, no shadow of a coast. Only water, from rim to rim of the world, darkening now like the inside of a closing eye. Whatever fresh water remains lies in a few skins and barrels, guarded as jealously as gold; thirst is held off mostly by the rains, when the skies consent to open. There is no way back. Behind lies the empire, its ships, its chains; ahead lies only the horizon, and hope, which costs nothing and is therefore all anyone owns.

Near you, under a patched tarp, your friend D lies sleeping, curled small against the cold spray, her breath rising and falling with the swell. You could wake her - it is her watch soon - but you do not. Let her sleep a little longer in this hour of bronze and violet light. You stand a moment longer at the gunwale, feeling the boat live beneath you, old and stubborn and unbroken.

Behind you the next rowers settle onto the benches, and the oars dip, and the strokes continue - patient, endless, like the heartbeat of a thing that refuses to die.

REWRITTEN PASSAGE 2 (dialog scene):

At last C's turn at the oars ends. He comes aft on aching legs and sinks down beside you, drawing a rough blanket over the two of you against the night chill. Together you look up. Between the frayed edges of the clouds the first stars are coming out, cold and fierce and far, the same stars that lit the halls your people abandoned and the roads they fled by. The boat rocks gently; the sea has gone soft and dark, whispering against the hull like an old woman telling secrets.

"Weed in the water today," says C. His voice is low, as if the sea might overhear and disprove him. "Fresh weed, torn off rocks, not old floating stuff. Green, and it smelled of the shore. And gulls at noon - two of them. Gulls do not come this far out unless there is land, not this far." He is silent a moment, watching the stars rather than you. "I think there is a coast south of us. I cannot promise it."

You say nothing. But under the blanket, where he cannot see, your hands have closed into fists - not from fear, but from something older and fiercer, the thing that has kept four friends and a hundred strangers rowing across an empty sea: the dream of land, the dream of freedom, the belief that the world has not forgotten you.

Perhaps it has not. Perhaps the weed and the gulls are true heralds, first messengers of an unknown shore. Or perhaps they are only weed, and only gulls, and C is a tired man seeing what he longs to see. Either way, there is only one direction left to believe in.

REWRITTEN PASSAGE 3 (action scene):

You reach for B. She has nothing left - the sea has taken the last of her strength, and her eyes, when they find you, are already half surrendered to the grey water. "Go," she gasps, the word barely living long enough to be born. It is her pride's last argument, and you refuse it.

You do not go. You lock your arm around her, iron and stubborn, and turn for the shore. The waves come at you like living walls, one after another without end, and you kick and pull, kick and pull, hauling her through the cold and the roar and the white fury of the surf. Progress comes by inches, hard-bought. Twice a breaking wave folds over you both and you swallow the sea - bitter, burning, choking - and twice you keep hold, because letting go is a thing that no longer exists in you. Her fingers dig into your shoulder; she holds on to you, and you hold on to her, and between those two holdings there is, for the moment, nothing in the world that cannot be survived.

The shore comes toward you - not quickly, not kindly, but it comes: a dark line, then shingle, then the crunch and drag of stones under your knees. Somewhere beyond the roar of the water, you are aware that this - an unnamed shore, a friend carried out of the sea - is how the old stories always begin. Let the tellers come later, if any ever do. For now there is only the ground under you, and B coughing salt and breathing, and that is enough.
