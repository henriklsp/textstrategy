// Sunrise wakes the five friends on the beach, wet and cold; they gather and count what they have; main decision.
The first rays of the sun wake you where you lie in the lee of the dunes, red through the last of the rain, finding your face among the sleeping.
Your clothes have not dried since the landing, and the cold of the night is still in them, and in the sand beneath you, and in your hands before your eyes are properly open.
The sun comes up out of the sea and gives the world its colours back one at a time: grey water, white surf, the dark line of the reef, and the boat lying on it, broken open, lifting and settling with every wave. Each wave takes something more off her, and the sound of her coming apart carries up the beach, patient and regular, the one voice the morning has.
One by one the four friends wake as well, stiff from the cold ground, shaking the sand from the two blankets, not one of them any warmer or any drier than you. <D> comes up out of sleep and looks from face to face before doing anything else. "Everyone all right? All five? Say so." <A> is on <A>'s feet before the rest, looking down the beach at the wreck.
Along the shore the other families are up too, digging their things out of the sand, mending what the sea left mendable, calling their children in from the water. They survived the same sea you did, and they have nothing to spare on anyone else's morning.
You call your friends together: <A>, <B>, <C> and <D>.
"Everything on the sand," says <A>. "Whatever the five of us own, we look at it together, and then we stop standing still."
So you lay it out in a ring between the blankets, and there is not much to lay out: <food amount>, <weapons amount>, <wood amount>, <silver amount>, a box of tools, two blankets. <B> reads each item out once, in a flat voice. <D> turns one of the blankets over, feeling for dry cloth, and does not find any.

The five of you take the tide line together, spread out between the dunes and the water, and pick the beach over stone by stone.

Far out on the reef the boat groans with every wave, coming apart in no hurry at all, and here and there along the surf the other families are bent to the same work, keeping their own finds to their own fires.
<C> walks the very edge of the water where the waves finish, because the sea leaves its gifts in patterns and <C> reads them: driftwood bleached grey and heavy with salt, thrown high past the tide line in the night.

§20
// Asking for help: no food is shared, but planks are for sale; no begging or yelling changes anything.
You walk the camps, from family to family, with the question out before you even arrive: help, anything, even a little.
The answer is the same everywhere, and it comes without unkindness.
A man mending a torn sail-cloth looks up and shakes his head before you finish. "I have children," he says. "Whatever the count is at your fire, it is smaller at mine. Ask the next one."
At the next fire a woman is boiling something that smells of almost nothing. She hears the whole question out, to the end, which is more than most do. "Yesterday I would have said yes," she says. "This morning I counted what we have, and I did not say yes to myself. I will not say it to you."
One woman, older than the rest, does not wait for the question at all. She names a price: two stacks of wooden planks, salvaged from the boat in the night by her family, for one silver coin.
"My sons dragged them out of the dark while your people slept," she says. "The wood is not mine, and it is not yours either. It belongs to whoever's hands are on it, and for one silver my sons' hands will let go."
There is nothing to beg with and nothing to shout with. You have tried words on the whole beach, and the whole beach has one answer, and it is a fair one: everyone here survived the same sea, everyone counts what is left every morning, and a beach full of people with nothing cannot afford to be generous.

// Making do: no help asked for, nothing searched for; the work of the day waits.
You look at what you have and decide it will have to be enough.
Nobody here is coming to save you. Nobody is waiting to be asked. The other families have their own counts to keep, and what is yours is what stands between you and the sea's patience.
So the five of you keep to yourselves and make do, and sit close around the box of tools in the lee of the dunes, out of the wind, and let the sun get at your clothes while it is in the mood.
"Every day on that boat I planned for land," says <B>. "It turns out land is also a thing one plans for. A man could sit here a week and the work would still be standing there, waiting to be chosen."
<C> is watching the green line past the dunes, away from the sea for the first time in weeks. "There is food out there," <C> says. "Growing wild, the way food grows when nobody is watching it. We have tools, hands, and a whole day. We do not own enough for despair."
"Then let us decide how to spend it," says <A>. "The day will not wait for us to feel ready."

§40
// The work focus: the main strategic decision of the day.
The friends do not agree on what the day is for, and the disagreement arrives at once.
"Food first," says <C>. "Hunger does not wait for walls. There is food out there for anyone who goes looking, and I am going looking."
"And when the weather turns again?" says <B>. "Tonight, or in ten days, it will turn. We were lucky once, and luck does not keep. Build first. What we raise this morning is what stands between us and the next storm."
"Food is also what stands between us and the next storm," says <C>. "The shorter one."
<A> listens to all of it, and then says the thing both of them already know. "Both are right. That is what is wrong with today. Whatever we choose, we choose a thing and lose a thing. A day spent foraging is a day nothing grows out of the sand. A day spent building is a day the food does not gather itself. Every hand given to one thing is missing from another."
<D> looks from one face to the next, and then at you. "Then let us choose it together and carry it together," <D> says. "A day the five of us do not agree on is worse than a day spent wrong."
They look to you. They will go where the day is sent.
