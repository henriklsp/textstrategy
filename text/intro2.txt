§1

The sun is going down into the western sea, and the water drinks its fire until only embers remain.
This is the last boat. She is old, her hull is tired, and she carries more than she was ever meant to carry: refugees and everything they own, cooking pots and sleeping children, the living freight of a drowned hope. The sail was destroyed days ago — the storm took it the way a thief takes a purse, quickly, leaving no way to argue — and since then everyone rows, turn upon turn, and the oars keep their slow heartbeat on the water.
You are tired in a way that has stopped being a feeling and become a place you live. The fresh water is nearly gone; there is only what the rain gives, and the rain has been mean. There is no land in sight. There is no way back.
By your bench your three friends rest from their turns at the oars: <A>, <B>, and <C>. Your fourth friend, <D>, sleeps beneath a tarp, worn hollow. You decide not to wake <D>. Let the sleeping sleep; the sea owes them that much.
Your own turn at the oars is over. Behind you, the next rowers settle into their seats, and the strokes go on, patient as a pulse.

§5
You stand at the side and watch the dark come on. The sea is black and moves like something breathing: slow swells that lift the boat and set it down again, like a giant passing a child from hand to hand. Far off, lightning flickers inside the clouds — silent light, too far away to hear, the way trouble often is. No land breaks the horizon in any direction. The world is water, and dark, and you.

§6
You check the food sacks, untying and tying again, counting what should not need counting. Grain, some dried fish, hard bread gone harder. There is little enough — a few days, if everyone eats little and no one eats first. You tie the sacks down again, carefully, as if carefulness were a kind of food.
#7 Check the water barrel
#10 Sit down among the others

§7
You look into the water barrel and see the bottom looking back. Little enough. The rain barrel is half full — the one mercy the sky has shown in days — and the clouds are gathering again tonight, heavy and dark and maybe generous, maybe not. You have learned to stop praying for weather and simply take what falls.
#5 Look at the sea again
#10 Sit down among the others

§10
<A>'s turn at the oars ends at the same time. <A> drops down beside you, takes a swallow of rainwater from the shared barrel, and looks out west, where the sun is finishing. <A> is not tired — or will not show it, which on this boat comes to the same thing.
"The gods did not carry us this far to drown us," says <A>. "We rose against the Dragon Emperor knowing what it would cost, and the gods watched us do it. They will not waste us on the sea. There is a land ahead. I feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound. And when we reach it, it will be ours. We will be the founders of something great. They will tell stories about us, and our names will be in them, and no emperor will ever own them again."
<A> looks out over the water and does not blink, as if the land might surface if <A> only refuses to look away.
What do you say?

"Yes," says <A>. "The storm took our sail, but it did not take us. Nothing out here is stronger than what we already survived." Behind you the oars keep their heartbeat, the boat moves on through the dark water, and for a moment the sea feels less like a prison and more like a road.

"I think they opened a door and pushed us through it," <A> says at last. "What we do on the other side is ours. That is the whole point of us. A door is not a promise. It is a dare."

§20

"I am done," <B> finally says. "Not tired-done. Done all the way through."

<B> looks at <A> across the boat — at all that undimmed certainty — and then at you.

§30
Last, <C>'s turn at the oars ends. <C> sits down with you, wraps a blanket around the two of you like a shared coat, and looks up at the sky, where the first stars come out between the clouds, shy as survivors.
"I have been counting the days," says <C>. "And the ships. Three left home together. The storm took the Wave Dancer. The Emperor's warship took the Dawn Chaser — but her people were pulled onto this boat, so the sea did not get them all." <C> pauses, and the names of the ships go out over the water like something given to the deep. "And I have been watching the stars. If the maps I remember are right, there should be a coast somewhere south of us. If they are wrong, there is nothing. But the birds went south yesterday, all of them, and the water is greener now than it was. Birds do not fly over nothing. Green water is shallow water."
<C> pauses again. "I think we are close. I cannot promise it. The stars keep their promises slowly."

§34
You ask what the stars truly said — not the hope drawn from them, but the words. <C> is quiet for a long moment. "That we are past the middle of the voyage. That the wind has been against us longer than it has any right to be. That my hands remember maps my eyes never saw." <C> shrugs under the blanket. "The stars do not speak, friend. §35
You ask whether the omens can be wrong. <C> laughs softly — a dry sound, like wind over sand. "Every time. That is why they are called omens and not promises." <C> looks at the clouds closing in from the west, and for a moment the eyes that count stars are just tired eyes. "But tonight I would take even a wrong omen over none at all. A wrong star is still a star."

§40
The wind picks up, and the evening changes its mind about being gentle. Clouds swallow the last light; the stars go out as if a hand had been passed over them. The oars bite harder, and the boat begins to pitch and lean like a thing that remembers being afraid. The families wake and hold their children. Someone says they see lightning, far out at sea, and no one answers, because there is nothing to answer.
You look at <C>. <C> is not counting stars anymore. <C> is listening to the wind, the way you listen to a door when someone is coming up the steps.

§41
You take a bucket and get in line with the bailers. The sea comes over the side faster than tired arms can throw it back; the deck grows slick, and the bundles groan as the boat rolls. You bail and bail, and the ocean waits, patient, for you to stop. Your arms burn the way the last light burned, and go out the same way.

When you touch the tarp, <D> stirs, half-wakes, holds the smallest child tighter, and sleeps again. There is nothing you can do here that sleep is not already doing. You move on.

§50
The storm arrives in full darkness, like everything important on this voyage: too late, and all at once. Rain hammers the deck. The waves take the boat and throw it, and the boat, old thing, takes it. People shout. Somewhere a child is crying, and someone is singing to the child, and both sound equally desperate.
Then a cry goes up from the front — one word that cuts through everything: "REEF!"
The boat grinds over the rocks with a sound like a living thing dying. The hull tears open. People and belongings spill into the black water together, and the water does not care which is which.

You reach for <A>, and <A> takes your arm with a grip that refuses everything the sea has to offer. §54
"TOGETHER!" you roar into the storm, until your voice breaks. "HOLD TOGETHER!" And the dark answers: voices take up the word, and hands find hands, and somewhere a rope finds a wrist. The line of survivors swims on as one animal with a hundred hearts, and the sea, for the moment, does not get to divide the herd.
#60 Continue to the shore

§60
You crawl out of the surf onto wet sand and fall down, and the sand holds you like a mother who will not ask questions yet. Around you others stumble out of the dark: mothers calling names into the roar, men dragging bundles that have forgotten what they were, an old man on his knees with both hands in the sand as if checking that it is real. One of the refugees lies moaning on the beach, badly hurt, and the others gather around like a wall against the weather. <D> is helped ashore, still half-asleep — awake enough to walk, not to talk.
Behind you, the wreck settles on the reef. The boat is broken — but it is still there, groaning with every wave, refusing, even now, to finish.
One thing from the wreck is still within reach, washed against the rocks, going once.
